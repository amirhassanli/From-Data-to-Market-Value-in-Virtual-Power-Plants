[First Paragraph] From Data to Market Value in Virtual Power Plants: A Structured Evidence Review of Decision Integrity, Uncertainty, and Electricity–Carbon Market Integration
[Heading 1] Abstract
[First Paragraph] Virtual power plants (VPPs) turn dispersed flexibility into market value through steps usually studied one at a time: measurement, forecasting, uncertainty modeling, optimization, bidding, clearing, dispatch, deviation, settlement. This review treats that chain as the unit of analysis. We ask: when a VPP study reports a better forecast, a richer optimization model, or a higher simulated profit, what evidence shows the decision would have held at decision time, and that the value survived clearing and settlement? Six search blocks, combined into five retrieval queries on OpenAlex, returned 2,670 records (1,668 after de-duplication); 39 studies were verified against publisher metadata and coded on a common evidence matrix. From that coding we build Data-to-Market Decision Integrity (DMDI), a seven-dimension instrument with explicit coding rules and four evidence levels. The pattern is consistent, and the two weakest dimensions are not merely rare but unassociated with the strong ones: information-set, decision, and market integrity are well evidenced (22/39, 32/39, and 31/39 explicit), while chronological out-of-sample validation, settlement realism, and reproducibility are rare (2/39, 4/39, and 0/39 explicit). We read that asymmetry, rather than any algorithmic shortfall, as the binding constraint on deployable value. The review is structured and PRISMA-informed: identification counts are complete, screening was purposive with its sensitivity measured, and the DMDI ratings come from a single coding pass pending independent double-coding. DMDI and the companion Data-to-Market Maturity Ladder are offered as diagnostic tools for authors and reviewers, not as validated external standards.

[Heading 1] 1. Introduction
[First Paragraph] Virtual power plants (VPPs) have become an important coordination paradigm for aggregating heterogeneous distributed energy resources (DERs), including renewable generation, battery energy storage systems (BESS), flexible commercial and industrial loads, electric vehicle (EV) fleets, and other controllable assets. The attraction of the VPP is not aggregation itself but the conversion of dispersed physical flexibility into coordinated operational and market value. This conversion increasingly depends on a data-intensive chain: measurements define the physical state of the portfolio, forecasts anticipate uncertain supply and demand, optimization converts information into operating schedules and bids, and market-clearing and settlement mechanisms ultimately determine whether the planned value is realized [1], [2], [3].
[Body Text] Recent reviews illustrate the breadth of this evolution. Ruan et al. [4] present a data-lifecycle perspective on VPP energy management spanning data creation, communication, decision support, privacy, learning methods, market participation, open datasets, and real-world projects. Gao et al. [5] focus on resource coordination and multidimensional VPP interaction. Kaiss et al. [6] survey VPP/virtual-aggregator concepts, architectures, and operation strategies. Esfahani et al. [7] map the transition from theoretical VPP models to real-world platforms and pilots, explicitly highlighting a persistent theory–practice gap. Zare et al. [8] propose a systematic market-coupling taxonomy classifying VPP interactions with electricity, flexibility, and carbon markets. These contributions establish a strong foundation, but they also expose a methodological question that has not, to our knowledge, been addressed as the central organizing question of a dedicated review: when a VPP study reports an improved forecast, a more sophisticated optimization objective, or a higher simulated profit, what evidence demonstrates that the resulting decision would have been valid at the time it was actually made, and that its value survives market clearing, physical deviation, and financial settlement?
[Body Text] This review addresses that question by treating VPP operation as an end-to-end decision chain — Information → Forecast → Uncertainty → Decision → Bid → Clearing → Dispatch → Deviation → Settlement → Realized Value — rather than as a set of independently evaluated modules. A forecasting model may have low error but provide little decision value; a stochastic optimization model may be mathematically sophisticated but rely on information unavailable at the decision time; a bidding strategy may show high in-sample profit while failing under temporally out-of-sample market conditions; and settlement-aware, market-realistic models may show smaller apparent gains while providing stronger evidence of deployability.
[Body Text] Building on this chain, the review introduces Data-to-Market Decision Integrity (DMDI) as a synthesis construct — not an established standard — organizing evidence along seven dimensions: information-set integrity, uncertainty integrity, decision integrity, market realism, out-of-sample validity, settlement realism, and reproducibility. The purpose is not to rank algorithms by a single score but to expose where evidence is present, partial, or absent along the decision chain, and to make that coding auditable by a third party.
[Heading 2] 1.1 Research questions
[First Paragraph] RQ1: How are data-driven VPP energy-management systems structured across the data-to-market decision chain? RQ2: How are uncertainty, dependence, and tail behavior represented and transferred from forecasting and scenario generation into VPP decisions? RQ3: To what extent does reported forecasting or AI performance translate into improved operational, bidding, and market outcomes? RQ4: How rigorously do existing VPP studies preserve information-set integrity, temporal causality, market timing, and out-of-sample validity? RQ5: How are electricity-market and carbon-market decisions coupled, and how do uncertainty and information quality propagate into economic and carbon outcomes? RQ6: What is the maturity of existing VPP research, from data-driven simulation to market-realistic, settlement-aware, and field-deployable operation?
[Heading 2] 1.2 Contribution and scope of claims
[First Paragraph] The contribution is fourfold. First, the review consolidates previously fragmented strands of VPP research into a common data-to-market decision chain. Second, it proposes DMDI as an auditable evidence-coding instrument, with explicit per-dimension coding rules (Section 5), rather than a conceptual taxonomy alone. Third, it separates forecast quality (FQ), decision quality (DQ), and realized market value (MV) as distinct, non-interchangeable evidence layers. Fourth, it develops a Data-to-Market Maturity Ladder distinguishing conceptual work from synthetic simulation, historical-data study, temporal out-of-sample study, market-realistic modeling, settlement-aware modeling, pilot validation, and operational deployment.
[Body Text] We are explicit about what this manuscript does not claim. It does not report an exhaustive PRISMA exclusion cascade (Section 2.4): the retrieval is executed and counted, but screening of the full frame was purposive, and we report the measured consequence (35 of 39 coded studies recovered) rather than a claim of completeness. It does not report inter-coder reliability statistics (Sections 2.7 and 11.6), and it does not claim that DMDI is a validated instrument outside this review. These boundaries are stated here so that every subsequent claim in the paper can be read against them.
[Heading 2] 1.3 Organization of the paper
[Body Text] Section 2 sets out the protocol, reports the executed retrieval, and says plainly where it stops short of a completed PRISMA review. Section 3 positions the work against earlier surveys, and Section 4 explains how the six retrieval blocks map onto the five synthesis streams. Section 5 defines DMDI and its coding rules; Section 6 adds a second, deliberately separate instrument for decision integrity, together with the uncertainty taxonomy, the decision-paradigm comparison, and the electricity–carbon coupling archetypes that later sections rely on. Sections 7–10 report the evidence, Section 11 converts it into a research agenda and answers the research questions in order, and Sections 12–13 close with limitations and conclusions.
[Heading 1] 2. Review Protocol
[Heading 2] 2.1 Review design and reporting standard
[First Paragraph] The review is designed as a structured literature review and reported in alignment with PRISMA 2020 [9] to the extent that the executed work supports it. PRISMA 2020 specifies a 27-item reporting checklist and a flow diagram for identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion [9]. We use PRISMA as a reporting scaffold: where a stage of the flow has been executed, its counts are reported in PRISMA form (Section 2.4); where a stage has not been executed, we say so rather than estimate it. Identification is executed; screening is not (Section 2.4). The manuscript is therefore presented as a structured, PRISMA-informed evidence review rather than as a fully executed PRISMA systematic review. This is a deliberate framing choice (Section 2.9), not a cosmetic one: it prevents the manuscript from claiming a degree of database-level reproducibility that it does not have.
[Heading 2] 2.2 Research questions and eligibility
[First Paragraph] Eligibility follows the research questions in Section 1.1. Studies are eligible when they are English-language, peer-reviewed journal papers or substantive peer-reviewed conference papers with direct relevance to VPP/virtual-aggregator energy management, scheduling, dispatch, bidding, flexibility coordination, uncertainty management, market participation, or deployment. Studies are excluded when VPP relevance is peripheral, energy-management evidence is absent, the contribution is a short abstract only, technical detail is insufficient for structured coding, or the record is a duplicate. Preprints are retained only as contextual leads unless independently verifiable against the criteria in Section 2.6. The OpenAlex frame itself was restricted to journal articles and reviews (Table I); conference papers and other document types therefore enter the corpus only through reference tracing from retrieved works, and the 39 verified-core studies are all journal articles or reviews.
[Heading 2] 2.3 Databases and explicit search strings
[First Paragraph] The retrieval was executed in OpenAlex on 30 October 2026, against title and abstract fields, restricted to 2018–2026, English-language journal articles and reviews. OpenAlex was chosen because the search can be re-executed by any reader without a subscription; the same strings can be run in Scopus, Web of Science, or IEEE Xplore where access exists, but those three were not executed for this version and no count is reported for them (Table I). The review window is 2018–2026, with pre-2018 studies retained only as flagged foundational seed references for bidding formalism or architecture. The retrieval strategy is organized into six overlapping search blocks so that a single vocabulary choice (e.g., “data-driven VPP”) does not exclude studies using adjacent terminology such as “virtual aggregator,” “flexibility platform,” or “stochastic bidding.” The blocks and their exact Boolean strings are:
[Body Text] Block 1 — Core VPP / aggregation terminology: ("virtual power plant" OR "VPP" OR "virtual aggregator" OR "flexibility aggregator")
[Body Text] Block 2 — Energy management, scheduling, and dispatch: ("energy management" OR "scheduling" OR "dispatch" OR "self-scheduling" OR "unit commitment")
[Body Text] Block 3 — Uncertainty and risk representation: ("uncertainty" OR "stochastic programming" OR "robust optimization" OR "distributionally robust" OR "polynomial chaos" OR "chance-constrained" OR "conditional value-at-risk" OR "CVaR" OR "risk-averse")
[Body Text] Block 4 — Data-driven and learning-based methods: ("deep learning" OR "reinforcement learning" OR "multi-agent reinforcement learning" OR "data-driven" OR "digital twin" OR "federated learning")
[Body Text] Block 5 — Electricity-market participation: ("bidding" OR "electricity market" OR "day-ahead market" OR "intraday market" OR "balancing market" OR "ancillary service" OR "reserve market" OR "imbalance settlement" OR "pay-as-bid" OR "pay-as-clear")
[Body Text] Block 6 — Carbon and environmental-instrument coupling: ("carbon market" OR "carbon trading" OR "carbon emission flow" OR "green certificate" OR "emission allowance")

[Heading 2] 2.4 Study identification, screening, and the evidence-integrity boundary
[First Paragraph] Identification is complete and is reported in full. The five retrieval queries returned 2,670 records, of which 1,002 were duplicates, leaving a de-duplicated frame of 1,668 works (Table I, Fig. 1). Screening is not complete. The frame was not read end to end at title and abstract level, so we do not report an exclusion cascade of the form PRISMA 2020 expects [9], and we do not present the frame as a screened population. Selection of the analytical corpus was purposive: works were drawn from the frame, and from citation chasing around the seed reviews [4], [8], when they addressed a VPP decision problem closely enough to be coded against the evidence matrix in Section 5. Thirty-nine studies were then verified against publisher metadata and carried through structured coding (Section 2.7). We call this set the verified core; it is the analytical corpus for every quantitative statement in Sections 7–10.
[First Paragraph] Purposive selection has a cost, and it is better measured than glossed. Re-running the five queries against the DOI identifiers of the 39 coded studies returns 35, a sensitivity of 90%. Every miss is explainable, and none is random. Two are reviews [5], [7] whose titles name virtual power plants but whose abstracts contain none of the energy-management, uncertainty, AI, market, or carbon terms, so no block intersection can reach them; block-intersection designs under-retrieve review literature almost by construction. The other two are primary studies [10], [11] that never use the VPP label in a title or abstract, one of which also has no abstract in the database. The same mechanism accounts for review and policy records cited elsewhere in the paper but outside the coded core ([12], [13], [14]). One further record, [15], looked like a miss for a different reason: the DOI printed in our reference list resolved to an unrelated paper, and with the corrected DOI the study is returned by four of the five blocks (Section 2.6). Two cheap remedies follow and both are in the research agenda (Section 11.6): a seventh block for review, policy, and regulatory vocabulary, and forward and backward citation chasing for every included study rather than only for the seed reviews.
[Body Text] We therefore describe the corpus precisely as 39 verified studies, selected purposively from a de-duplicated frame of 1,668 works, rather than as a population sample or a final systematic-review inclusion set. Its composition is reported study by study in Tables VIII and XI, and the coding rules that produced it are in Section 5.
[Body Text] Fig. 1 shows the resulting flow in PRISMA-aligned box form. The identification and de-duplication boxes carry executed counts that a reader can reproduce from Table I. The selection box is marked as purposive because that is what it was, and it feeds eligibility and inclusion directly; no record appears as excluded at screening, because no screening step was run that would justify an exclusion count. The figure should be read as identification-complete and selection-disclosed, not as a completed systematic-review cascade.

[Heading 2] 2.5 Data extraction fields
[First Paragraph] Each verified-core study is coded for: VPP architecture; DER composition; decision horizon; data source; forecasting method; uncertainty representation; dependence/scenario treatment; optimization paradigm; bidding mechanism; market products; carbon-integration level (Section 9.1); information-set definition; temporal split; out-of-sample testing; baseline quality; settlement model; computational reporting; reproducibility; deployment evidence; and Data-to-Market Maturity level (Section 10). A reference-verification status (Section 2.6) is maintained as a separate field so that bibliographic confidence is never conflated with methodological relevance.
[Heading 2] 2.6 Reference verification protocol
[First Paragraph] Every cited record was checked against at least one authoritative source — publisher metadata, a DOI resolver, or an indexing service — using a three-part check: (i) a metadata match for title, author list, venue, volume, and year; (ii) independent bibliographic cross-consistency across at least two records where available; and (iii) claim-to-source alignment, i.e. that any quantitative claim attributed to a source is actually present in it. Metadata checks were performed against Crossref DOI records and publisher landing pages. Records that could not be matched were removed before submission rather than retained with a caveat. Bibliographic verification is maintained as a coding field separate from methodological relevance (Section 2.5), so that bibliographic confidence is never conflated with methodological relevance.
[Heading 2] 2.7 Structured coding roles and AI-assisted coding disclosure
[First Paragraph] Literature identification, reference verification, and first-pass evidence coding for this review were structured around eight predefined analytical coding roles, used to divide the audit into tractable, cross-checked sub-tasks: (A) VPP concepts and architecture; (B) uncertainty, risk, and bidding; (C) data-driven and AI methods; (D) electricity, flexibility, and carbon markets; (E) deployment and practical barriers; (F) the complete decision chain; (G) evidence quality and validation; (H) reference verification. These are coding roles applied with the assistance of a large language model under direct researcher supervision, not independent human reviewers or a claim of formal inter-rater methodology; every output produced under a role was reconciled by the authors into a single study-level evidence matrix, and every reference appearing in the final list (Section 14) was individually cross-checked against a primary bibliographic source before inclusion (Section 2.6). This disclosure is made in accordance with IEEE author guidelines on the use of AI-generated content in submitted articles.
[Body Text] We flag explicitly, as a limitation rather than an omission, that this structured-coding process has not yet been validated by independent double-coding (e.g., a second, blinded human coder reproducing the DMDI ratings in Table XI and computing agreement such as Cohen’s κ). Reporting that the literature shows low explicit reproducibility (Section 10) while not yet demonstrating the reproducibility of our own coding process would be an internal inconsistency if left unaddressed; we therefore treat single-coder DMDI ratings as provisional and indicative, name double-coding as a required next step (Section 11.6, Section 12), and avoid any composite scoring that would present the current ratings as more settled than they are.
[Heading 2] 2.8 Risk-of-bias / evidence-quality layer (distinct from DMDI)
[First Paragraph] DMDI (Section 5) codes what evidence a study provides along the decision chain; it is not a risk-of-bias instrument and does not assess study quality independent of the chain. We therefore define a second, deliberately separate evidence-quality layer, adapted from PRISMA’s expectation that a systematic review report how study-level quality was assessed [9], summarized here as Q1–Q8 in Table II:

[Heading 2] 2.9 Reporting status of the evidence synthesis
[First Paragraph] This review is reported as a structured evidence review rather than as a completed PRISMA 2020 systematic review. Four elements of a systematic review are now reported in full: the eligibility criteria (Section 2.2), the executed retrieval with its record counts (Sections 2.3–2.4, Table I), the sensitivity of that retrieval against the analytical corpus (Section 2.4), and the reference-verification procedure (Section 2.6). Three elements remain incomplete and are stated as limitations rather than estimated: exhaustive title and abstract screening of the 1,668-record frame; independent double-coding of the DMDI ratings (Sections 2.7 and 11.6); and retrospective re-tagging of the existing not-reported entries into NR-R and NR-U (Section 5.3). Where a reported quantity depends on one of these unfinished steps — notably the per-dimension counts in Table XI — it is described as indicative of a pattern rather than as a prevalence estimate.

[Heading 1] 4. Synthesis Streams and Retrieval Blocks
[First Paragraph] The six retrieval blocks in Section 2.3 exist to maximize recall during search (so that uncertainty, AI, market, and carbon vocabulary are each retrieved even when a study does not use the phrase “VPP energy management” directly). They are a search-execution device, not a synthesis structure. For synthesis, the verified core is organized into five thematic streams, which is the level at which Sections 7–10 report results:
[Compact] Concept/architecture — VPP definitions, DER composition, coordination structures.
[Compact] Uncertainty/risk — stochastic, robust, DRO, and risk-averse representations and their calibration.
[Compact] Data-driven/AI — forecasting, deep learning, reinforcement learning, and hybrid learning–optimization methods.
[Compact] Market/carbon — electricity-market products, settlement mechanisms, and carbon/green-certificate coupling.
[Compact] Practical deployment — pilot, hardware-in-the-loop, and field-operational evidence.
[First Paragraph] Every verified-core study is tagged to at least one synthesis stream independently of which retrieval block(s) originally surfaced it, since a study retrieved via the carbon block, for example, may be synthesized primarily under uncertainty/risk if that is its main methodological contribution.

[Heading 2] 5.1 The decision chain and seven dimensions
[First Paragraph] VPP decision-making is treated as a continuous chain: Information → Forecast → Uncertainty → Decision → Bid → Clearing → Dispatch → Deviation → Settlement → Realized Value (Fig. 2). DMDI comprises seven evidence dimensions along this chain:
[Compact] Information-set integrity (I) — whether the study uses only information genuinely available at the decision gate being modeled.
[Compact] Uncertainty integrity (U) — whether uncertainty distributions, dependence, scenarios, and tails are empirically justified rather than merely assumed.
[Compact] Decision integrity (D) — whether the optimization or learning policy is evaluated under its own stated constraints, without look-ahead bias.
[Compact] Market realism (M) — whether market products, timing, clearing rules, and strategic assumptions correspond to a defined, named market setting.
[Compact] Out-of-sample validity (O) — whether performance is evaluated on chronologically unseen conditions rather than random splits or in-sample data.
[Compact] Settlement realism (S) — whether realized value is computed through a credible settlement mechanism (deviations, imbalance penalties/rewards), rather than the ex-ante objective value alone.
[Compact] Reproducibility (R) — whether data provenance, parameters, baselines, and evaluation procedures are reported in enough detail for independent reconstruction.
[First Paragraph] Deployment maturity is tracked as a companion field, P (Section 10), because operational evidence cannot be inferred from methodological sophistication alone.
[Heading 2] 5.2 Explicit coding rules
[First Paragraph] Each dimension is coded on a four-level evidence status: Explicit, Partial, NR-R (not reported), or NR-U (not verifiable from the available evidence, e.g., because full text was inaccessible or the claim could not be checked against a primary source). Coding rules, illustrated using out-of-sample validity as the worked example:
[Compact] O = Explicit if all three hold: (i) a chronological train/validation/test split or rolling-window evaluation is used; (ii) the test period is disjoint from and later than any period used for fitting/tuning; (iii) quantitative performance is reported specifically on that held-out period.
[Compact] O = Partial if temporal separation is mentioned but at least one of (ii)–(iii) is missing or ambiguous (e.g., the split is described only qualitatively, or results pool in-sample and out-of-sample periods).
[Compact] O = NR-R if the paper does not discuss temporal separation at all and does not claim otherwise.
[Compact] O = NR-U if the paper’s description is too incomplete (e.g., abstract-only access) to determine which of the above applies.
[First Paragraph] Analogous rules apply to the other six dimensions, substituting the relevant chain-link criteria — for example, S = Explicit requires that reported profit/cost figures are computed after applying a stated deviation or imbalance-settlement rule (not the optimization objective value alone), and R = Explicit requires that a reader could, in principle, reconstruct the reported experiment from the paper’s stated data source, parameters, and evaluation protocol without contacting the authors.

[Heading 2] 5.3 On NR-R vs. NR-U
[First Paragraph] “Not reported” conflates several different situations: a study may be silent on a dimension, or the dimension may not be determinable from the accessible text. The distinction is applied to all coding performed for this review. Disaggregating the NR entries already present in the Sections 7–10 tables into NR-R versus NR-U would require re-reading each of the affected primary sources against the two-way rule; this re-tagging pass has not yet been completed (Section 11.6). Table XI (Section 10) therefore reports a single combined NR column for the existing 39-study matrix, with the caveat stated here, while all tables introduced with the four-level scheme (e.g., Table VIII) use it directly.
[Body Text] DMDI is intentionally not collapsed into a single numerical score. This avoids arbitrary dimension weights and prevents strong evidence on one dimension from masking a critical gap on another. The preferred presentation throughout is a dimension-by-dimension evidence matrix (Fig. 5) supplemented by the maturity ladder (Section 10).
[Heading 1] 6. Decision Integrity: A Complementary Four-Layer Framework
[Heading 2] 6.1 Two instruments, two questions
[First Paragraph] This review uses two deliberately separate instruments, because they answer two different questions and rest on two different evidence bases. DMDI (Section 5) is an evidence-coding instrument applied per study along the data-to-market decision chain: it answers “what evidence does this specific paper provide?” The Decision Integrity framework introduced in this section is a conceptual threat-and-countermeasure taxonomy applied at the level of the literature as a whole: it answers “independent of what any given paper reports, what could cause a market-facing VPP decision to be wrong, unstable, or untrustworthy, and how mature are the available defenses?” Threats such as corrupted telemetry, adversarially perturbed forecasts, opaque bidding agents, and unverifiable carbon accounting are rarely the explicit subject of a bidding or scheduling paper, yet they bound how much confidence the field should place in a paper's reported value even when its DMDI ratings are strong.
[Body Text] Decision Integrity is therefore defined as the property that a VPP's market-facing decisions (bids, offers, schedules, and control setpoints) remain correct (consistent with true system state and market rules), robust (stable under uncertainty and distributional shift), and trustworthy (verifiable, explainable, and auditable) despite imperfect, incomplete, or adversarially manipulated inputs. It is organized into four layers — data, model, execution, and governance — each with representative threats and countermeasures rated by maturity. Table IV is a qualitative, literature-wide judgment by the authors, informed by the cybersecurity, robust-optimization, privacy, and market-design literatures cited throughout this section; it is not a re-coding of the 39-study verified core and does not carry the same evidentiary status as the DMDI tables in Sections 7–10 (Section 12).
[Body Text] The two instruments are complementary rather than overlapping: DMDI’s Information-set (I) and Reproducibility (R) dimensions correspond most closely to the Data (L1) and Governance (L4) layers below; DMDI’s Uncertainty (U) and Decision (D) dimensions correspond most closely to the Model layer (L2); and DMDI’s Market (M) and Settlement (S) dimensions correspond most closely to the Execution layer (L3). Where DMDI shows a literature-wide gap (Section 10.1: O, S, and R are weak), the Decision Integrity layers below make explicit why such gaps are consequential and what would close them.

Solution technologies cut across layers: decomposition methods such as ADMM [53] and Benders decomposition [54] support scalable, privacy-preserving coordination (L1/L4), while game-theoretic tools — Nash equilibrium analysis [55] and Shapley-value surplus sharing [56] — support execution-layer (L3) monitoring of strategic behavior in coupled or thin markets.

[Heading 2] 6.5 Enabling technologies and their integrity/maturity trade-offs
[First Paragraph] Four technology families recur across the Decision Integrity literature, each trading value for a distinct integrity cost:
[Compact] Reinforcement learning for forecasting and bidding. RL and deep-learning bidding agents, including hierarchical schemes for aggregated heterogeneous microgrids [65], adapt to nonstationary prices and behavior that static scenario sets miss, but they are comparatively opaque and can be destabilized by adversarial or out-of-distribution inputs [43]; constrained and explainable RL variants are an active bridge technology.
[Compact] Distributed optimization and decomposition. ADMM-based coordination [53] and Benders decomposition [54] let large, heterogeneous DER portfolios be optimized without a single centralized model of every asset, simultaneously improving scalability, privacy (L4), and resilience to single points of failure — a rare case where a value-driven technique also improves integrity.
[Compact] Blockchain and smart-contract settlement. Ledger-anchored logs of offers, meter readings, and carbon-credit transfers make ex-post verification cheap [48], directly supporting L3/L4 auditability; throughput, energy cost, and the “oracle problem” (a ledger cannot verify off-chain meter truth on its own) keep maturity medium–low, a verdict shared by review-level assessments of blockchain integration in VPPs [13].
[Compact] Digital twins and federated/privacy-preserving learning. High-fidelity replicas of VPP portfolios support pre-market stress-testing of bidding policies under simulated attack or drift [47]; federated learning [49] allows prosumer-level models to train without raw-data egress, addressing the privacy tension in L4 governance.
[First Paragraph] The pattern across these technologies mirrors the DMDI finding in Section 10.1: technologies closest to raw market value (forecasting, RL bidding) matured first, while integrity-enabling technologies (explainability, ledger-anchored audit, federated/privacy-preserving coordination) remain comparatively immature — the same information-to-settlement and execution-to-governance imbalance, seen here from a technology-maturity angle rather than a per-study evidence angle.
[Heading 1] 7. Results: Information and Forecast Integrity
[First Paragraph] Across the verified core, information-set integrity (I) is the dimension most consistently reported: 22 of 39 studies (56%) provide explicit information-set definitions (measurement timestamps, forecast publication time, or market gate closure), and a further 17 provide partial evidence, with no study coded NR on this dimension. This is consistent with the fact that most bidding and scheduling formulations must, by construction, state what is known at the bidding stage even if they do not audit it against real-world timing.
[Body Text] Forecast integrity is treated in this review as an intermediate metric, not an endpoint. Recent forecasting work addresses data scarcity through transfer-learning and adversarial graph mechanisms for distributed PV [21], long-horizon recursive forecasting via BiLSTM-based decision support [23], and explicit uncertainty reduction from day-ahead to balancing markets [22]. Work on the demand side, such as data-mining-driven incentive design for aggregated response [66], improves the input layer in much the same way. These studies strengthen the forecasting layer, but their contribution to realized market value must be judged by whether the forecast improvement changes the resulting bidding decision under the same information set — the FQ → UQ → DQ → MV distinction used throughout this review (Section 1). A forecasting paper reporting lower MAE without a demonstrated operational or bidding consequence is classified as predictive evidence only, not market-value evidence.
[Heading 1] 8. Results: Uncertainty, Decision, and Market/Settlement Integrity
[Heading 2] 8.1 Uncertainty and decision integrity
[First Paragraph] The literature shows a clear methodological progression from deterministic scheduling toward stochastic programming (scenario trees) [18], [67], including robust stochastic multi-energy dispatch [68], through interval, two-stage, and price-maker robust variants [69], [70], [71], and day-ahead bidding formulations for renewable portfolios [72], to distributionally robust optimization (DRO), increasingly Wasserstein-metric-based and dominant since approximately 2023 [24], [15], [25], [73], including Wasserstein scheduling formulations [74]. Decision integrity (D) is well reported overall (32/39 explicit, 7/39 partial, 0 NR), reflecting that most optimization-focused papers do specify their constraint sets; market realism (M) is similarly strong (31/39 explicit, 8/39 partial). Uncertainty integrity (U) is explicit in 23/39 studies and partial in 15/39, with one NR.
[Body Text] However, methodological sophistication in uncertainty representation does not by itself establish uncertainty validity. Three distinctions matter. First, uncertainty representation differs from uncertainty calibration: a Gaussian or empirical distribution can be embedded in an optimization model without demonstrating that it reproduces the relevant error distribution. Second, marginal accuracy differs from dependence integrity: separately calibrated wind, PV, load, and price distributions may fail to reproduce cross-variable and temporal dependence. Third, in-sample risk control differs from out-of-sample risk control: a risk measure such as CVaR is decision-relevant only when the loss distribution used for evaluation reflects the conditions encountered after the bid or schedule is fixed.

[Heading 2] 8.3 The Optimization-to-Settlement Evidence Gap
[First Paragraph] The single most consequential pattern in Sections 8.1–8.2, in our reading, is that the chain Objective value → Cleared value → Delivered value → Settled value is rarely reported in full. Of the verified core, most uncertainty-handling and market studies, including two-step spot-market formulations [78], report objective-function or cleared-market value; substantially fewer report the value that would remain after physical deviations and settlement penalties are applied (S explicit in only 4/39 studies). This is not evidence that reported gains are wrong — many studies explicitly and reasonably target theoretical upper-bound performance rather than deployment-readiness — but it means that a reported “profit improvement” should be read, by default, as an objective-value or cleared-value claim unless the study explicitly states otherwise. We treat this Optimization-to-Settlement Evidence Gap as a central, citable finding of this review (see also Section 10.1) rather than an incidental observation (Fig. 3), and recommend that future studies report which of the four stages their headline number corresponds to.

[Heading 2] 9.2 The remaining review-level gap
[First Paragraph] Taken together with [8], [16], [33], [63], [64], and [79],, the evidence indicates that primary research on electricity–carbon coupling is accelerating faster than review-level synthesis of its methodology. We identified Wang et al. (2025) as the closest carbon-focused review, which examines carbon-flow modeling and electricity–carbon transactions; we did not identify one focused specifically on settlement-valid carbon-value evidence, dynamic emission-factor treatment, or the interaction between carbon-price volatility and electricity-bidding uncertainty. This remains, in our assessment, the most defensible and currently supported gap this review can claim in its carbon-market discussion.
[Heading 2] 9.3 A sharper statement of the gap: the missing VPP-level two-market model
[First Paragraph] The evidence in Sections 9.1–9.2 supports a more specific version of the carbon gap, worth stating explicitly because it converts a general observation (“carbon coupling is under-reviewed”) into a concrete, buildable research target. At the generation-fleet and market-design level, work on the coupling between carbon-trading and tradable-green-certificate mechanisms under electricity marketization shows that these two instruments interact through investment behavior and price formation in ways that a single-instrument model misses [80], and this fleet/policy-level coupling logic is structurally the same two-market problem — jointly manage an energy position and a carbon-related position under correlated uncertainty — that a VPP-level model would need. At the aggregator level, by contrast, the coding in Table X shows that most carbon-integrated studies treat the carbon price as a fixed scalar in the objective (archetypes A1–A2, Section 6.4; coupling levels 1–2, Section 9.1) rather than as a traded, uncertain position; explicit allowance buy/bank/sell decisions alongside the energy bid, with carbon price treated as a full uncertainty object rather than a constant, are essentially absent from the verified core. The missing piece is therefore not a new algorithm — two-market stochastic and DRO programming already exists in the fleet/policy-coupling literature [33], [15], [80], and market-trigger scheduling has been explored at the VPP level [81], and border-carbon-pricing analysis shows the same “price on a subset of the generation mix” logic extends naturally to trade-exposed operation [82] — but its transplantation to the aggregator, where the portfolio is smaller, the assets are behind the meter, and the carbon-displacement claim is only as credible as the metering that supports it (Section 6.2, L1). This framing of the gap is corroborated by convergent findings across the electricity–carbon literature [8], [80], which strengthens confidence that it reflects a structural gap rather than an artifact of this review's search strategy or verified core.
[Heading 1] 10. Results: Maturity, Out-of-Sample Validity, and Reproducibility
[Heading 2] 10.1 Central finding: information-to-settlement validation, not algorithmic sophistication, is the binding constraint
[First Paragraph] We propose the following as the central finding of this review, directly supported by the verified-core coding: the principal weakness of contemporary VPP research is not the absence of advanced optimization or learning methods, but the incomplete validation of the information-to-settlement pathway. Within the verified core, out-of-sample validity is explicit in only 2/39 studies (partial in 5, NR in 32); settlement realism is explicit in only 4/39 (partial in 18, NR in 17); reproducibility is explicit in 0/39 studies (partial in 38, NR in 1). By contrast, decision integrity and market realism are each explicit in over 75% of studies. This asymmetry — strong evidence at the modeling layer, weak evidence at the validation and settlement layer — is consistent with, and reinforces, the theory–practice gap identified independently in [7]. The same asymmetry can be read the other way round: the literature compares VPP decision-method families far more often on revenue than on auditability, which mirrors, from a different angle, this review's own Optimization-to-Settlement Evidence Gap (Section 8.3).
[Body Text] We restate the caveat from Section 2.7 here because it is directly relevant to this finding: these counts come from a single structured-coding pass that has not yet been independently double-coded, so they should be read as indicative of a pattern, not as a precise, externally validated prevalence estimate. We consider the direction of the pattern (O and R far weaker than I, D, and M) more robust than the exact counts, because it recurs consistently across the market/settlement (Table IX), carbon (Table X), and AI/forecasting (Table XII) evidence groupings independently.

[Heading 2] 10.3 The Data-to-Market Maturity Ladder
[First Paragraph] To compare heterogeneous studies without collapsing methodological novelty and validation maturity into one axis, we use an eight-level ladder (Table XIII). The ladder describes the strength of empirical validation and deployment evidence, not the scientific quality of an algorithm in isolation — a Level 1 conceptual DRO formulation is not thereby a weaker piece of mathematics than a Level 6 heuristic pilot; it simply carries different evidence for deployment claims.

[First Paragraph] The sharpest expression of the same result is a single empty cell. The two studies explicit on O ([75], [17]) and the four explicit on S ([67], [15], [35], [10]) are disjoint: not one of the thirty-nine studies reports both chronological out-of-sample validation and settlement realism (Fig. 7b). A field can live with a rare capability. What it cannot do is demonstrate the end-to-end performance claim in Section 10.1 through any single study, because the two halves of that claim are never measured together.
[First Paragraph] We also tested whether the situation is improving. It is not. Spearman's ρ between publication year and the number of explicit dimensions is −0.07 (p = 0.67), so the recent half of the corpus is no more complete than the older half, even though 72% of the retrieved frame dates from 2023 onward (Section 2.4). The one field that drifts in the expected direction is deployment maturity, and only weakly (ρ = 0.26, p = 0.11). Whatever is driving the growth in VPP output over this window, it is not a secular improvement in reporting completeness.
[First Paragraph] Three limits apply to everything in this subsection. The counts describe a fixed corpus of thirty-nine studies, not a sample from a population, so the exact tests are read as statements about exchangeability within the corpus rather than as inferences about the literature; with n = 39 the design can detect only large associations, and a non-significant pair means “not large here”, not “absent”. The fifteen comparisons are exploratory, which is why both exact p and Benjamini–Hochberg q are reported and why we lean on the two surviving associations rather than on any single borderline one. And the ratings themselves come from a single coding pass (Section 2.7), so a double-coded matrix could move individual cells; the structural findings above rest on the pattern across many cells, which is more robust to that than any single count.

[Heading 2] 11.1 Evidence bottlenecks in the data-to-market chain
[First Paragraph] Reading Tables XI–XIII together produces a consistent picture: information (I), decision (D), and market (M) integrity are comparatively strong; out-of-sample validity (O), settlement realism (S), and reproducibility (R) are comparatively weak, with uncertainty integrity (U) in between. This is not an artifact of any single evidence table — it recurs in the uncertainty/decision comparison (Table VIII), the market/settlement/carbon comparison (Tables IX–X), and the AI/forecasting comparison (Table XII) independently. The joint-structure analysis in Section 10.4 sharpens the claim: O and S are not merely rare, they are unassociated with the I/D/M cluster, and no study in the core is explicit on both. This pattern also mirrors the L1–L4 maturity ratings of the complementary Decision Integrity framework (Section 6.2): DMDI’s O/S/R weaknesses correspond closely to the Execution- and Governance-layer gaps identified there. We treat closing the O/S/R gap, rather than further algorithmic refinement of I/D/M, as the most consequential direction for the field (Section 11.2–11.6).
[Heading 2] 11.2 Information-set integrity as a reporting requirement
[First Paragraph] Future studies should explicitly define the information available at every decision point — measurement timestamps, forecast publication times, market gate closure, asset availability, and prices observed before bidding — ideally via a decision-time information table or equivalent machine-readable description. This directly addresses look-ahead bias and makes temporal causality auditable by a third party.
[Heading 2] 11.3 From uncertainty representation to uncertainty validation
[First Paragraph] Stochastic, robust, DRO, and risk-averse formulations should be evaluated as much by their empirical uncertainty-model quality — calibration, coverage, dependence preservation, temporal correlation, tail behavior, scenario-reduction distortion, and out-of-sample risk — as by their mathematical treatment of uncertainty.coverage, dependence preservation, temporal correlation, tail behavior, scenario-reduction distortion, and out-of-sample risk.
[Heading 2] 11.4 From AI accuracy to decision and market value
[First Paragraph] AI studies should report a hierarchy of evidence — predictive metrics, operational metrics, bidding metrics, realized market metrics — and prefer temporal hold-out tests to random splits for time-dependent market data. Regime-shift tests and ablations are especially valuable when AI is embedded in optimization or bidding.
[Heading 2] 11.5 Settlement realism and an expanded benchmark metric set
[First Paragraph] VPP market studies should represent the actual sequence of market decisions relevant to the modeled jurisdiction, and where imbalance settlement is material, should report scheduled energy, delivered energy, deviations, penalties, and settlement prices as distinct quantities (Section 8.3). We propose the following reporting set (Table XV):

[Heading 2] 11.6 Reproducibility and coding-reliability roadmap
[First Paragraph] Beyond study-level reproducibility (open data, code, and parameterization — Section 5.1), we identify the review’s own coding reproducibility as an explicit, near-term task: (i) repeat the executed retrieval in Scopus, Web of Science, and IEEE Xplore and screen the combined frame exhaustively, so that the purposive selection in Section 2.4 can be replaced by a full exclusion cascade; (ii) recruit a second, blinded coder to re-apply the DMDI and Q1–Q8 instruments to the verified core and report agreement (e.g., Cohen’s κ or Krippendorff’s α) per dimension; (iii) retrospectively re-tag existing NR entries as NR-R or NR-U (Section 5.3); (iv) only after (i)–(iii), consider whether any numerical DMDI summary beyond the per-dimension counts in Table XI is warranted. Until then, this review presents its own quantitative statements with the same caution it applies to the primary literature it reviews.
[Heading 2] 11.7 Electricity–carbon coupling beyond objective-function aggregation
[First Paragraph] Carbon integration should be reported at the six accounting levels in Section 9.1 and positioned against the six coupling archetypes in Section 6.4, not merely as present/absent. Level 5–6 evidence (carbon-market clearing and settlement-coupled decisions) and Archetype A4–A6 mechanisms provide the strongest support for claims about coupled electricity–carbon value, because market timing, constraints, prices, and settlement interact directly with electricity-market decisions.
[Heading 2] 11.8 Answers to the research questions
[Body Text] RQ1 asked how VPP energy-management systems are structured across the chain, and the verified core answers it unevenly. Studies are strongest in the middle, where forecasting meets optimization and a bid is formed, and thin at both ends: few describe how measurements reach the model, and fewer still describe what happens after clearing. No coded study reports all ten links as one auditable sequence.
[Body Text] RQ2 asked how uncertainty, dependence, and tail behavior travel from forecasting into the decision. Representation travels well; calibration does not. Twenty-three of 39 studies state an uncertainty model explicitly, but dependence and tail behavior are usually asserted rather than reproduced from data, and the distortion introduced by scenario reduction is almost never quantified.
[Body Text] RQ3 asked whether forecast or AI performance translates into operational and market outcomes. For the most part it is not shown to. Predictive evidence and market-value evidence remain two largely separate literatures: studies report error metrics and stop, or report profit and never connect it to a change in the forecast.
[Body Text] RQ4 asked how carefully studies preserve information-set integrity, temporal causality, market timing, and out-of-sample validity. This is where the spread is widest. Information-set definitions are the best-reported dimension (22/39 explicit) and chronological out-of-sample evaluation the worst (2/39), so most reported performance rests on splits that a market would not have offered.
[Body Text] RQ5 asked how electricity and carbon decisions are coupled, and how uncertainty propagates between them. Coupling is real but shallow. Most studies reach objective, constraint, or market-participation coupling, few model a traded carbon position whose price is itself uncertain, and where coupling runs deep it is usually sequential rather than joint.
[Body Text] RQ6 asked about maturity. The verified core sits at Levels 1–4 of the ladder. Settlement-aware evidence is rare, pilot evidence rarer, and operational evidence absent, which is what most limits any claim about deployable value.
[Heading 1] 12. Limitations
[First Paragraph] Several limitations qualify every claim in this review. First, identification is executed but screening is not (Section 2.4). Table I reports what each query returned on 30 October 2026, and the de-duplicated frame of 1,668 works is a number any reader can reproduce; what we cannot offer is a record-by-record exclusion trail through that frame, because selection was purposive. The measured cost is a retrieval sensitivity of 35 of 39 for the coded core: two of the four misses are reviews whose abstracts carry none of the stream terms, and two are studies that never use the VPP label. Those are the numbers a reader should use to discount every claim in Sections 7–10, and they are the reason the paper is framed as a structured, PRISMA-informed review rather than a completed systematic review.
[First Paragraph] Second, structured coding has not yet been independently double-coded; the DMDI and maturity-ladder counts reported in Sections 7–10 should be read as indicative of a pattern rather than as externally validated prevalence statistics, and closing this gap is item (ii) of the research agenda (Section 11.6). Third, the NR-R/NR-U distinction introduced in Section 5.3 has been applied only prospectively; the existing 39-study matrix (Table XI) reports a combined NR column pending a re-tagging pass.
[First Paragraph] Fourth, the complementary Decision Integrity framework introduced in Section 6 is a conceptual, literature-wide threat-and-maturity taxonomy, not a per-study coded instrument like DMDI; its layer-maturity ratings (Table IV) are illustrative judgments by the authors rather than counts derived from the verified core, and should not be read as having the same evidentiary status as the DMDI tables in Sections 7–10. Fifth, heterogeneity in VPP definitions, market structures, datasets, horizons, and evaluation objectives limits direct meta-analysis for many technical metrics; the review therefore emphasizes structured evidence synthesis over pooled effect sizes.
[First Paragraph] Sixth, coverage — while spanning architecture, uncertainty, AI, market, and carbon streams — is not exhaustive for a 2018–2026 window; in particular, European market-design variants, probabilistic scenario-generation methodology, and hardware-in-the-loop validation studies are comparatively under-represented in the current verified core and are natural targets for the completed database screening.
[First Paragraph] Seventh, DMDI, the Decision Integrity framework, and the maturity ladder are proposed by this review and are not established external standards; their purpose is to provide an auditable structure for comparing evidence quality across heterogeneous VPP studies, not to rank individual papers or algorithms (Section 12.1). Eighth, the reference list was verified against Crossref and publisher DOI records, and every entry resolves to a real, independently locatable publication; verification of metadata is nevertheless a point-in-time check, since volume, issue, and page details for very recent articles can change between online-first and issue publication.
[Heading 2] 12.1 Boundary conditions for interpretation
[First Paragraph] The DMDI dimensions are designed to expose missing evidence and support transparent comparison across heterogeneous studies; they do not imply that every VPP application requires the same market products, settlement mechanism, or validation depth. Regulatory scope differs widely across jurisdictions [12], so a coding profile that looks adequate in one market can be silent on obligations that another market imposes. The maturity ladder describes the strength of empirical validation and deployment evidence, not the scientific quality of an algorithm in isolation — a synthetic-simulation study using a genuinely novel formulation and a settlement-aware pilot study using a comparatively simple heuristic occupy different rungs of the ladder for reasons that have nothing to do with which is the better piece of applied mathematics.
[Heading 1] 13. Conclusion
[First Paragraph] VPP research is increasingly data-driven, uncertainty-aware, AI-enabled, and market-oriented. The consequential question is whether an improvement survives the complete data-to-market chain. This review shifts the unit of analysis from the isolated algorithm to decision integrity: what information was available, how uncertainty was represented, how the decision was produced, how the bid interacted with the market, how deviations were settled, and what economic or carbon value was realized.
[Body Text] Within the limits stated in Section 12 — chief among them, that screening of the retrieved frame was purposive rather than exhaustive and that structured coding has not yet been independently double-coded — the verified-core evidence supports a consistent pattern: information-set, decision, and market integrity are comparatively well reported in the 2018–2026 VPP literature, while out-of-sample validity, settlement realism, and reproducibility are comparatively weak. Electricity–carbon market coupling is an active but still comparatively under-reviewed methodological frontier, with primary research on joint bidding and carbon-price-linked strategy accelerating faster than dedicated review-level synthesis of its accounting and dynamic-pricing methodology. The proposed DMDI framework and Data-to-Market Maturity Ladder are offered as diagnostic, auditable tools — not validated external standards — toward a VPP research agenda that treats settlement-aware benchmarking, chronological out-of-sample evaluation, explicit carbon-accounting-boundary reporting, and coding-level reproducibility as first-class evidence, alongside continued algorithmic innovation.
